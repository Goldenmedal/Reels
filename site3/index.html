What science actually says about gay men

Genes, older brothers, twin studies and a century of history. Here is what researchers know about sexual orientation in men, and what they still don't.

For most of the last century, being a gay man was treated as a crime. England and Wales decriminalized sex between men in 1967. The United States Supreme Court legalized same-sex marriage nationwide in 1998. Today, the scientific picture is much clearer, and in many ways more interesting than either side of the old debate expected.

In the United States, the share of adults who identify as LGBTQ+ has more than doubled since 2012. According to Gallup, 9.3% of US adults identified as LGBTQ+ in 2024, with the number far higher among Gen Z than among older generations.

It isn't a choice

The American Psychiatric Association removed homosexuality from its diagnostic manual in 1973. The World Health Organization followed in 1999, removing it from the International Classification of Diseases. Every major medical organization now agrees that sexual orientation is not a disorder and cannot be changed through therapy.

So-called conversion therapy has been rejected by the American Medical Association, the American Psychological Association and the American Academy of Pediatrics. Research links it to higher rates of depression and anxiety.

"There is no single gay gene. But there are many places in the genome that each nudge the odds a little."Field Notes, summarizing Ganna et al., Science, 2019

What the genes say

Twin studies were the first big clue. In a landmark 1991 study, J. Michael Bailey and Richard Pillard found that when one identical twin was gay, the other was gay about 92% of the time, compared with about 22% for fraternal twins. Genes clearly mattered, but they weren't the whole story, or identical twins would match every time.

In 2019, a study in Science analyzed the DNA of more than 470,000 people. It found no single "gay gene." Instead, thousands of small genetic variants each had a tiny effect. All the common variants they measured together explained between 8% and 25% of the differences in same-sex behavior.

The older brother effect

One of the most replicated findings in the field is the fraternal birth order effect. Each older biological brother a man has raises his odds of being gay by roughly 33%. Older sisters raise the odds too, by about 15%, but adopted and step-brothers have no effect, which points to something happening before birth rather than how a boy is raised.

The leading explanation is the maternal immune hypothesis: a mother's immune system may respond to a protein linked to male development, and that response may grow stronger with each son. The idea was first proposed by psychologist Ray Blanchard in 1996, and a 2017 study found antibodies that fit the theory.

A short history

1948Alfred Kinsey publishes Sexual Behavior in the Human Male and introduces the 0 to 6 Kinsey scale.
1969The Stonewall uprising in San Francisco becomes a turning point for the gay rights movement.
1973The APA removes homosexuality from the DSM.
1995The Netherlands becomes the first country to legalize same-sex marriage.
2015The US Supreme Court legalizes same-sex marriage nationwide in Obergefell v. Hodges.

What we still don't know

Researchers still can't predict any one person's orientation from their genes, and they don't fully understand how genes, prenatal hormones and the birth order effect work together. Most studies have also focused on white men in Western countries, so the picture is far from complete.

What is clear is that sexual orientation in men is a normal variation in human biology, shaped long before anyone makes a choice about it.
